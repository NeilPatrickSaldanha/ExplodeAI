# Primary sources split RAG into two meanings

*Pass 1 evidence report for an AI engineering lesson. All sources accessed 2026-10-18. Synthesized from three research-note files: `rag_foundations.md`, `chunking_embeddings.md`, `retrieval_grounding.md`.*

The primary record uses "Retrieval-Augmented Generation" in two different senses, and a lesson has to keep them apart. In the original paper, Lewis et al. (NeurIPS 2020) describe a specific jointly trained model: a DPR bi-encoder retriever over a FAISS index of **21M 100-word Wikipedia passages** feeds a **BART-large generator**, and the retrieved passage is treated as a latent variable marginalized over the top-K, in two variants called RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). The pipeline that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google document today is looser. You chunk documents, embed the chunks, index them (often alongside BM25), retrieve the top-K, optionally rerank, and paste the results into the prompt of a model that was never trained with the retriever ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval); [Google Cloud RAG Engine](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/rag-engine/rag-overview)). The building blocks are well documented by their original sources: BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza 2009), reciprocal rank fusion with k = 60 (Cormack et al. 2009), HNSW as an *approximate* search method, FAISS flat indexes as *exact* brute-force search, and the bi-encoder versus cross-encoder trade-off (SBERT). The most quotable numbers, however, are provider defaults or vendor-run evaluations. OpenAI's **800-token chunks with 400-token overlap**, Anthropic's **35% / 49% / 67%** cuts in retrieval failures, and its "up to 30 percent" gain from putting the query last all belong to particular products and dates, so they should be taught as "X reports" rather than as general laws. Two failure layers are documented by primary sources. Retrieval can miss the right chunk: Anthropic's best configuration still had a 1.9% top-20 failure rate. The model can also misuse a chunk it was given: Lost in the Middle showed accuracy falling to below closed-book levels when the answer sat mid-context ([arXiv 2307.03172 v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03172v3)). No primary source claims that RAG eliminates hallucination. Many excerpts came through a summarizing fetch tool and are flagged for a Pass 2 character-level check.

**How to read the evidence.** The note-takers used two collection methods. Some text was read in full from raw sources: the OpenAI embeddings guide as markdown, the arXiv API abstract for SBERT, the Robertson & Zaragoza PDF via text extraction, sentence-transformers source code, the RRF paper PDF, the Lost in the Middle v3 PDF, and the Anthropic docs pages. ## Lewis et al. defined RAG as a jointly trained 626M-parameter model

The original RAG paper, arXiv 2005.11401, has four versions: v1 (22 May 2020), v2 (7 Dec 2020), v3 (29 Mar 2021) and v4 (12 Apr 2021, current). Its comments field reads "Accepted at NeurIPS 2020" ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)). The abstract describes **two memories**: "the parametric memory is a pre-trained seq2seq model and the non-parametric memory is a dense vector index of Wikipedia." It frames the contribution as "pre-trained models with a differentiable access mechanism to explicit non-parametric memory" ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)). The motivation is that parametric-only models "cannot easily expand or revise their memory." The fix is a generator that "conditions on these latent documents together with the input to generate the output" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)).

The components are concrete. The retriever "is based on DPR," which "follows a bi-encoder architecture." The authors "use a pre-trained bi-encoder from DPR to initialize our retriever and to build the document index," and that bi-encoder was itself trained with retrieval supervision on Natural Questions and TriviaQA. The generator is **BART-large**, "a pre-trained seq2seq transformer … with 400M parameters." The two DPR encoders have "110M parameters each," "making a total of 626M trainable parameters" (Appendix G) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). The non-parametric memory is the **December 2018 Wikipedia dump**, "split into disjoint 100-word chunks, to make a total of 21M documents." It is stored as "a single MIPS index using FAISS" with "a Hierarchical Navigable Small World approximation for fast retrieval." The paper frames top-K lookup as "a Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) problem, which can be approximately solved in sub-linear time" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). So the founding RAG system already used approximate search.

The two variants differ in how often the model may switch documents. **RAG-Sequence** "uses the same retrieved document to generate the complete sequence" and "treats the retrieved document as a single latent variable that is marginalized" "via a top-K approximation." **RAG-Token** lets the model "draw a different latent document for each target token" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). The abstract describes RAG-Sequence as conditioning "on the same retrieved passages across the whole generated sequence" ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)). Training used "k∈{5,10}," with test-time k "set … using dev data." RAG-Sequence decoding comes in "Thorough Decoding" and "Fast Decoding" forms, and RAG-Token test results use "15 retrieved documents" (Appendix A) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)).

The training design depends on one asymmetry. The authors "keep the document encoder (and index) fixed, only fine-tuning the query encoder" and the BART generator, because "updating the document encoder … is costly as it requires the document index to be periodically updated" (Section 2.4) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). Freezing the document side meant the 21M-passage index only had to be built once, and that made end-to-end training practical. This is the note-takers' inference, not a stated claim. The same property supports the paper's knowledge hot-swap result: "we can update RAG's world knowledge by simply replacing its non-parametric memory" (Section 4.5). Reported results are that RAG "set the state-of-the-art on three open domain QA tasks" and generates "more specific, diverse and factual language than a state-of-the-art parametric-only seq2seq baseline" ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)). Appendix C notes that the code was later "ported … to HuggingFace Transformers," which is the Hugging Face link to the original model ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)).

### DPR supplied the retriever, the corpus and the dot-product convention

Dense Passage Retrieval (Karpukhin et al., arXiv 2004.04906) has three versions: v1 (10 Apr 2020), v2 (2 May 2020) and v3 (30 Sep 2020, current). It is marked "EMNLP 2020" ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)). DPR is a "simple dual-encoder framework." It uses "two independent BERT" encoders and takes "the representation at the [CLS] token as the output." The authors "choose the simpler inner product function" for similarity because the alternatives they tried performed comparably (the reason is paraphrased by the tool). DPR splits each article "into multiple, disjoint text blocks of 100 words" from the "Dec. 20, 2018" dump, "which results in 21,015,324 passages in the end." It indexes them with FAISS, "an extremely efficient, open-source library for similarity search and clustering of dense vectors," and trains with in-batch negatives, "an easy and memory-efficient way to reuse the negative examples" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.04906)). The headline result is that DPR "outperforms a strong Lucene-BM25 system largely by 9%-19% absolute" in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)). **Wording conflict:** ar5iv returned "greatly" instead of "largely" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.04906)). The numbers agree, so quote the arxiv.org/abs wording. Because RAG's index matches DPR's corpus setup, RAG very likely reused DPR's passage set. Neither paper states that match in an extracted quote, so it remains an inference.

Pass 1 left some gaps here. The quotes have not been character-checked against the NeurIPS proceedings PDF. The RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token probability formulas (Section 2.1) were not extracted, nor were the HNSW/FAISS index parameters. DPR's per-dataset accuracy table and its BM25 hard-negative details are also missing.

## Modern providers describe a retrieve-then-paste pipeline

All three providers now describe RAG as a workflow the developer assembles, or one the provider hosts. None describes a jointly trained model. **Anthropic's** "Introducing Contextual Retrieval" ("Published Sep 19, 2024") gives the most compact definition: "RAG is a method that retrieves relevant information from a knowledge base and appends it to the user's prompt" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Its steps are: "Break down the knowledge base … into smaller chunks of text," "usually no more than a few hundred tokens." Then "Use an embedding model to convert these chunks into vector embeddings that encode meaning," then "Store these embeddings in a vector database that allows for searching by semantic similarity." In parallel, "Use BM25 to find top chunks based on exact matches." Finally, "Add the top-K chunks to the prompt to generate the response" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). The post was fetched at anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval. Whether anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval is now the canonical URL was not checked.

**Google Cloud's** RAG Engine overview (cloud.google.com redirects 301 to docs.cloud.google.com; "Last updated 2026-10-16 UTC") gives the clearest six-stage list and suits a canonical lesson diagram. **Ingestion** means "Ingest data from different data sources." In **transformation**, "data is split into chunks." **Embedding** produces "Numerical representations of words or pieces of text." **Indexing** "structures the knowledge base so it's optimized for searching." **Retrieval** is to "find information that is relevant to the query." In **generation**, "the retrieved information becomes the context added to the original user query" ([Google Cloud RAG Engine](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/rag-engine/rag-overview)). Google's motivation is that LLMs "don't understand private knowledge, that is, your organization's data," and the stated benefit is that "the model can reduce hallucinations and answer questions more accurately." **Naming flag:** the page now reads "RAG Engine, a component of Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform," not "Vertex AI RAG Engine." It also mentions an allowlist requirement (the sentence was truncated by the tool) and says "Data residency and AXT security controls aren't supported." No default chunk size is stated on the overview page.

The **Gemini API** offers a hosted equivalent. "The Gemini API enables Retrieval Augmented Generation ("RAG") through the File Search tool." Files are "automatically broken down into chunks, embedded, indexed, and uploaded" ([Gemini File Search](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/file-search); "Last updated 2026-10-13 UTC"). **OpenAI** documents the same hosted pattern. File search lets models "retrieve information in a knowledge base of previously uploaded files through semantic and keyword search" ([OpenAI File search](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-file-search)). "Vector stores are the containers that power semantic search for the Retrieval API and the file search tool" ([OpenAI Retrieval](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval)). The developer flow is: upload a file to the Files API, create a vector store, add the file, poll until it shows "completed," then pass `file_search` and `vector_store_ids` to the Responses API (paraphrased by the tool). platform.openai.com/docs/guides/tools-file-search now redirects 301 to developers.openai.com. OpenAI's embeddings guide includes a section titled "Question answering using embeddings-based search" but never uses the term "RAG" ([OpenAI Embeddings](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)).

Anthropic differs on one structural point. Its docs leave retrieval to the developer and give two ways to bring results in: "From tool calls: Your custom tools return search results, enabling dynamic RAG applications," or "As top-level content." Search result blocks exist for "RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) applications where Claude needs to attribute answers to your documents" ([Claude Docs: Search results](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/search-results)). "No beta header is required," with Claude Haiku 3 as the stated exception. The notes found no Anthropic-hosted vector store comparable to OpenAI's. Anthropic also documents when to skip RAG altogether: "If your knowledge base is smaller than 200,000 tokens (about 500 pages of material)," "you can just include the entire knowledge base in the prompt," with prompt caching making this cheaper ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)).

## Chunk sizes are product defaults, not research findings

No primary source names a universally best chunk size or overlap. Anthropic says only that "the choice of chunk size, chunk boundary, and chunk overlap can affect retrieval performance" [WF] ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). The specific numbers that circulate are each tied to a product or an example, and their units differ.

**OpenAI's default is 800 tokens with 400 overlap.** The Retrieval guide states: "By default, `max_chunk_size_tokens` is set to `800` and `chunk_overlap_tokens` is set to `400`," so "every file is indexed by being split up into 800-token chunks, with 400-token overlap between consecutive chunks" [WF] ([OpenAI Retrieval](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval)). The bounds are that `max_chunk_size_tokens` "must be between 100 and 4096 inclusive" and that overlap "should not exceed `max_chunk_size_tokens / 2`." The file limits are "512 MB" and "no more than 5,000,000 tokens per file." The API reference names the strategy: "If not set, will use the `auto` strategy," which "currently uses a `max_chunk_size_tokens` of `800` and `chunk_overlap_tokens` of `400`." A `static` strategy lets you "customize your own chunking strategy" [WF] ([OpenAI API reference: create vector store file](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/vector_stores/subresources/files/methods/create)). The word "currently" marks this as a default that can change. No page shows a date. The default overlap is 50%, the maximum the API allows, so nearly every token is indexed in two chunks (the note-takers' arithmetic). The File search tool page itself contains no chunking parameters. Older Assistants API v2 docs listed the same 800/400, but that API path is legacy.

**Google exposes chunking controls but no confirmed defaults.** Gemini File Search's `chunking_config` takes `max_tokens_per_chunk` and `max_overlap_tokens`. The examples use 200 and 20, which are **sample values, not stated defaults** (paraphrased by the tool) ([Gemini File Search](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/file-search)). Other documented limits: File Search is not available in the Live API, "cannot be used simultaneously with Grounding with Google Search," allows 100 MB per document, and stores are best kept under 20 GB. Indexing embeddings are billed, storage and query-time embeddings are free, and retrieved tokens are billed as context (paraphrased).

**Anthropic's contribution targets what chunking destroys, not chunk size.** Its diagnosis is that "traditional RAG solutions remove context when encoding information." The example is a chunk like "The company's revenue grew by 3%..." that names neither the company nor the period [WF]. The fix is "prepending chunk-specific explanatory context to each chunk before embedding" and before "creating the BM25 index ("Contextual BM25")." Claude 3 Haiku generates this context from the prompt "Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the overall document," and the result is "usually 50-100 tokens" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). The cost is "$1.02 per million document tokens," one-time, with prompt caching. That figure assumes "800 token chunks, 8k token documents, 50 token context instructions, and 100 tokens of context per chunk" [WF]. Overlap and contextual prepending fix related problems differently. Overlap preserves text that straddles a boundary. Contextualization adds document-level facts, such as a company name, that no neighboring chunk contains (the note-takers' inference).

Framework and community docs add splitter mechanics but carry less authority. LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter "tries to split on them in order until the chunks are small enough," with default separators `["\n\n", "\n", " ", ""]`. The aim is "trying to keep all paragraphs (and then sentences, and then words) together as long as possible." It notes that "Overlapping chunks helps to mitigate loss of information when context is divided between chunks." Size is measured in **characters** by default via `length_function` [WF] ([LangChain](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/integrations/splitters/recursive_text_splitter)). LlamaIndex's SentenceSplitter "attempts to split text while respecting the boundaries of sentences." Its example uses `chunk_size=1024, chunk_overlap=20`, but the page does not call these class defaults; the often-cited 1024/200 was not confirmed from source. Its SemanticSplitterNodeParser "adaptively picks the breakpoint in-between sentences using embedding similarity" [WF] ([LlamaIndex](https://developers.llamaindex.ai/python/framework/module_guides/loading/node_parsers/modules/)). The Hugging Face cookbook by Aymeric Roucher is undated community content, not official API docs. It warns that chunks "too small will truncate ideas, and too large will dilute them," sets overlap to one-tenth of chunk size, and notes that chunks longer than the embedding model's maximum sequence length (512 for `thenlper/gte-small`) are "truncated before processing, thus losing relevancy" [WF] ([HF Cookbook](https://huggingface.co/learn/cookbook/advanced_rag)). That last point is the one most worth teaching: a chunk larger than the embedder's input limit is silently cut. Lesson tables must not compare OpenAI tokens, LangChain characters and Lewis's 100 *words* as if they were the same unit.

## Normalized embeddings make cosine, dot product and L2 rank identically

The providers phrase the core property of embeddings with care, and a lesson should keep that care. OpenAI says "An embedding is a vector (list) of floating point numbers" and "The distance between two vectors measures their relatedness." It adds that "Small distances suggest high relatedness and large distances suggest low relatedness" ([OpenAI Embeddings](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)). Anthropic says "Text embeddings are numerical representations of text that enable measuring semantic similarity" ([Claude Docs: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)). SBERT says "semantically meaningful sentence embeddings that can be compared using cosine-similarity" ([arXiv 1908.10084](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)). The defensible teaching version is that texts the model treats as related *tend* to have nearby vectors under the chosen similarity function. That is a learned, task-dependent tendency, not a guarantee. No fetched source analyzes failure cases such as negation or numbers.

The SBERT paper (Reimers & Gurevych, arXiv 1908.10084; submitted 27 Aug 2019; EMNLP 2019 [WF]) explains why separate embeddings matter at all. Cross-encoding BERT "requires that both sentences are fed into the network, which causes a massive computational overhead." "Finding the most similar pair in a collection of 10,000 sentences requires about 50 million inference computations (~65 hours) with BERT." SBERT uses "siamese and triplet network structures," which cuts this "from 65 hours with BERT / RoBERTa to about 5 seconds with SBERT, while maintaining the accuracy from BERT" ([arXiv 1908.10084](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)). Only the abstract was read. "Bi-encoder" is the sentence-transformers docs' term; the paper says "siamese." The RAG relevance is that document vectors can be computed once, offline, so only the query is embedded at search time.

Provider specifics change fast and should be dated in the lesson. **OpenAI's** text-embedding-3-small and -large default to **1536** and **3072** dimensions. Both accept up to 8192 input tokens, with MTEB scores of 62.3% and 64.6% (ada-002: 61.0%). A `dimensions` parameter shortens vectors "without the embedding losing its concept-representing properties." A large embedding "can be shortened to a size of 256 while still outperforming an unshortened `text-embedding-ada-002`" on MTEB. Manual truncation requires renormalizing, and the guide links Matryoshka Representation Learning ([arXiv 2205.13147](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13147)) ([OpenAI Embeddings](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)). The guide says the v3 models "lack knowledge of events that occurred after September 2021," which describes the models' training data, not the page's date. The January 25, 2024 launch date comes only from third parties ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/ai-gateway/models/text-embedding-3-large/about); [Pinecone](https://www.pinecone.io/learn/openai-embeddings-v3/)), because the OpenAI launch post returned HTTP 403 ([OpenAI blog](https://openai.com/index/new-embedding-models-and-api-updates/)). Whether OpenAI has released newer embedding models or deprecated ada-002 as of 2026-10-18 is **unverified**.

**Google's** embeddings page ("Last updated 2026-10-09 UTC") lists "Stable: `gemini-embedding-2`" (latest update April 2026), described as the "first multimodal embedding model in the Gemini API." "Stable: `gemini-embedding-001`" (June 2025) "remains available" for text-only use [WF] ([Gemini Embeddings](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/embeddings)). gemini-embedding-001 takes task types including `RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT` ("Embeddings optimized for document search.") and `RETRIEVAL_QUERY`, plus `SEMANTIC_SIMILARITY`, `CLASSIFICATION`, `CLUSTERING`, `CODE_RETRIEVAL_QUERY`, `QUESTION_ANSWERING` and `FACT_VERIFICATION`. By contrast, "You cannot use the `task_type` field for the `gemini-embedding-2` model"; instructions go into the prompt text instead, e.g. `task: search result | query: {content}`. "By default, both models output a 3072-dimensional embedding," and Google recommends "768, 1536, or 3072." gemini-embedding-2 supports 128–3072 and "auto-normalizes truncated dimensions," while for gemini-embedding-001 "you must manually normalize non-3072 dimensions." Input limits are 8,192 tokens (embedding-2) and 2,048 tokens (embedding-001). File Search examples use `models/gemini-embedding-2` ([Gemini File Search](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/file-search)).

**Anthropic** states plainly: "Anthropic does not offer its own embedding model." It points to "Voyage AI by MongoDB" and advises that "you should assess a variety of embeddings vendors" ([Claude Docs: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings); docs.claude.com redirects 302 to platform.claude.com; no page date). The current recommendations are "`voyage-4-large`: Best quality," "`voyage-4`: Balanced performance" and "`voyage-4-lite`: Lowest latency and cost," plus voyage-code-4. Each has a 32,000-token context and 1024 dimensions by default (256/512/2048 optional). voyage-4-nano is open-weight and defaults to 2048 dimensions. voyage-context-4 is "For chunk-level and document-level retrieval," with 120,000 tokens when auto-chunking is on. voyage-multimodal-3.5 and the rerankers rerank-3 and rerank-3-lite are also listed, and voyage-3.x, voyage-law-2 and voyage-finance-2 are marked previous-generation or domain-specific. API keys are now created in MongoDB Atlas ("AI Model APIs") at ai.mongodb.com, so tutorials using dash.voyageai.com keys may be stale. The 2024 Contextual Retrieval post found "Gemini and Voyage embeddings to be particularly effective," but those were older model generations ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)).

The math lesson is the same across vendors. "OpenAI embeddings are normalized to length 1." "We recommend cosine similarity. The choice of distance function typically doesn't matter much." On unit vectors, "Cosine similarity can be computed slightly faster using just a dot product," and "Cosine similarity and Euclidean distance will result in the identical rankings" ([OpenAI Embeddings](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)). Voyage says the same: "Voyage AI embeddings are normalized to length 1," so "Cosine similarity is equivalent to dot-product similarity, while the latter can be computed more quickly" ([Claude Docs: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)). **Hugging Face sentence-transformers** (v6.1.0 on PyPI at access; the docs show no version) defaults to cosine and offers "cosine," "dot," "euclidean" and "manhattan." It notes that "Dot product on normalized embeddings is equivalent to cosine similarity" [WF] ([SBERT STS](https://sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/usage/semantic_textual_similarity.html); [SentenceTransformer API](https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/sentence_transformer/SentenceTransformer.html)). However, `normalize_embeddings` ("Whether to normalize returned vectors to have length 1.") **defaults to `False`**, verbatim from source ([sentence-transformers model.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/sentence-transformers/main/sentence_transformers/sentence_transformer/model.py)). So normalization is the developer's job when vectors are truncated by hand, when using gemini-embedding-001 below 3072 dimensions, and with many open models.

Several providers also embed queries and documents differently. Voyage instructs: "Do not omit `input_type` or set `input_type=None`." It prepends "Represent the query for retrieving supporting documents: " to queries and "Represent the document for retrieval: " to documents ([Claude Docs: Embeddings](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/embeddings)). "For all retrieval tasks and use cases (for example, RAG), use the `input_type` parameter." Gemini's RETRIEVAL_QUERY/RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT split serves the same purpose. A lesson should not imply that queries and documents are always embedded identically, or that "relevant" and "semantically similar" mean the same thing.

## Retrieval stacks exact or approximate search, BM25, rank fusion and reranking

### Exact search scores every vector; HNSW trades exhaustiveness for speed

FAISS's own wiki draws the exact-versus-approximate line. IndexFlatL2 is "the simplest version that just performs brute-force L2 distance search on them," and needs no training: "For `IndexFlatL2`, we can skip this operation" ([FAISS wiki: Getting started](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/Getting-started); last edited 28 Jun 2020). The index summary table labels IndexFlatL2 "Exact Search for L2" (comment "brute-force") and IndexFlatIP "Exact Search for Inner Product," "also for cosine (normalize vectors beforehand)." IndexHNSWFlat is "Hierarchical Navigable Small World graph exploration," with Exhaustive: "no" ([FAISS wiki: Faiss indexes](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/Faiss-indexes); last edited 28 Jul 2025; read from a flattened table). The metric page warns that METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT "is not by itself cosine similarity, unless the vectors are normalized." For cosine, "build an index with `METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT`," "normalize the vectors prior to adding them to the index (with `faiss.normalize_L2` in Python)," and "normalize the vectors prior to searching them" [WF] ([FAISS wiki: MetricType and distances](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/MetricType-and-distances); last edited 3 Dec 2024). The same page gives |x − y|² = 2 − 2⟨x, y⟩ for normalized vectors, so L2 and inner-product rankings agree (paraphrased). It adds that inner product "is typically used for maximum inner product search in recommendation systems." Forgetting to normalize either the stored vectors or the query breaks cosine ranking without raising any error, which makes it a good failure mode to teach.

The HNSW paper (Malkov & Yashunin, arXiv 1603.09320; v1 30 Mar 2016, current v4 14 Aug 2018; no journal ref on arXiv) says in its title that it is "approximate nearest neighbor search." HNSW "incrementally builds a multi-layer structure consisting from hierarchical set of proximity graphs (layers)," similar to earlier Navigable Small World graphs. "Starting search from the upper layer together with utilizing the scale separation boosts the performance compared to NSW" and "allows a logarithmic complexity scaling" [WF] ([arXiv 1603.09320](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320)). The logarithmic claim comes from the abstract. The paper body, its assumptions and its M/efConstruction/efSearch parameters were not read, so a lesson should say "the authors report," not state a proven bound. The FAISS paper (Johnson, Douze & Jégou, "Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs," arXiv 1702.08734, v1 28 Feb 2017) covers GPU brute-force, approximate and product-quantization search. Its abstract claims k-selection at up to 55% of theoretical peak and an 8.5× speed-up over the prior GPU state of the art (paraphrased). It reports a k-NN graph over "95 million images from the Yfcc100M dataset" in 35 minutes, and a 1-billion-vector graph in under 12 hours on 4 Maxwell Titan X GPUs (partly paraphrased) ([arXiv 1702.08734](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)). The 2024 "The Faiss library" paper (arXiv 2401.08281) was not consulted. In short, flat indexes return the true top-k, while ANN indexes trade some recall for latency. RAG's own FAISS-HNSW index sat on the approximate side.

### BM25 is saturating, length-normalized TF-IDF, and its common defaults come from Lucene

Robertson & Zaragoza's "The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond" (*Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval* 3(4), 2009, 333–389, DOI 10.1561/1500000019) is the primary source for BM25 ([R&Z 2009 PDF](https://www.staff.city.ac.uk/~sbrp622/papers/foundations_bm25_review.pdf)). The term weight is w_i^BM25(tf) = tf / (k1((1 − b) + b·dl/avdl) + tf) · w_i^RSJ (Eq. 3.15), which the paper calls "the classic BM25 term-weighting and document-scoring function," with "the full document score … obtained by summing these term-weights over the … set of query terms." Saturation means "any one term's contribution to the document score cannot exceed a saturation point (the asymptotic limit), however frequently it occurs in the document" (§3.4.2). For length normalization, "setting b = 1 will perform full document-length normalisation, while b = 0 will switch normalisation off" (Eq. 3.12). Without relevance information, the RSJ weight "reduces as before to a form of idf," so "the BM25 weight looks very much like a traditional tf*idf weight" apart from the saturating tf (§3.5). Anthropic frames it the same way: "BM25 works by building upon the TF-IDF … concept" [WF] ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)).

**The parameter values need careful attribution.** R&Z say "the model provides no guidance on how these should be set," and experiments "suggest that in general values such as 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k1 < 2 are reasonably good in many circumstances," with optimal values depending "on other factors (such as the type of documents or queries)." "A common combination would be b = 0.5 and k1 = 2," though "many experiments suggest a somewhat lower value of k1 and a somewhat higher value of b" (§3.5–3.5.1). The familiar **k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75** are Elasticsearch/Lucene defaults. Elasticsearch's docs say k1 "The default value is `1.2`" and b "The default value is `0.75`," with BM25 as the default similarity [WF] ([Elasticsearch similarity](https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/elasticsearch/index-settings/similarity)). Cite each number to its own source. PDF excerpts were reassembled after ligature-dropping extraction ("signicant") and need a check against the rendered PDF. The lexical weakness is stated by SBERT's docs: "It will not recognize synonyms, acronyms or spelling variations" [WF] ([SBERT Retrieve & Re-Rank](https://sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/retrieve_rerank/README.html)). No primary TF-IDF source (e.g. Spärck Jones 1972) was fetched.

### RRF fuses rankings without calibrating scores; Anthropic used "rank fusion" without naming RRF

BM25 scores and cosine similarities live on different scales, so hybrid systems fuse ranks rather than raw scores. Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher's "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods" (SIGIR '09, 19–23 Jul 2009, Boston, pp. 758–759, DOI 10.1145/1571941.1572114; DOI and pages from ir.webis.de/dblp search results, not the PDF) defines RRFscore(d) = Σ_{r∈R} 1/(k + r(d)), "where k = 60 was fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation" ([Cormack et al. 2009](http://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)). "The constant k mitigates the impact of high rankings by outlier systems," and the pilot "indicated that k = 60 was near-optimal, but that the choice was not critical." The reciprocal form keeps lower-ranked documents relevant, since their importance "does not vanish as it would were, say, an exponential function used." RRF "combines ranks without regard to the arbitrary scores returned by particular ranking methods." On TREC data it "outperforms Condorcet, CombMNZ and the best system by 4% to 5% on average" (MAP). The paper is honest about limits: on LETOR 3 "CombMNZ edges RRF by a small margin (p ≈ .2)," and on TREC 9 a human-in-the-loop run beat it.

Anthropic's hybrid pipeline uses "BM25 to find top chunks based on exact matches" and "embeddings to find top chunks based on semantic similarity," then "Combine and deduplicate results … using rank fusion techniques" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). **The post does not name RRF, so do not claim Anthropic used it.** OpenAI is explicit. Its Retrieval guide lets developers "control how reciprocal rank fusion balances semantic embedding matches vs. sparse keyword matches" via "`hybrid_search.embedding_weight` (`rrf_embedding_weight`)" and "`hybrid_search.text_weight` (`rrf_text_weight`)." It also offers a `ranker` such as "`auto` or `default-2024-08-21`," a `score_threshold` "between 0.0 and 1.0," and `rewrite_query=true` to "automatically rewrite your queries for optimal performance" [WF] ([OpenAI Retrieval](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval)). The same guide says search returns "10 results maximum by default, but you can set up to 50 using the `max_num_results` parameter" [WF]. **Conflict flag:** a separate note found no default `max_num_results` on the File search *tool* page, whose example shows 2. Whether the 10/50 figure applies to the `file_search` tool as well as the Retrieval API is unconfirmed.

### Cross-encoders rerank a bi-encoder shortlist

Hugging Face's sentence-transformers docs (footer "© Copyright 2026"; no version) define the two-stage pattern. "Bi-Encoders produce for a given sentence a sentence embedding," passing sentences "independently." "A Cross-Encoder does not produce a sentence embedding" and outputs "an output value between 0 and 1 indicating the similarity of the input sentence pair." "Cross-Encoder achieve better performances than Bi-Encoders" but scale badly: clustering 10,000 sentences needs about 50 million pair scores, "which takes about 65 hours," against "only 5 seconds" for bi-encoder embeddings [WF] ([SBERT Cross-Encoder](https://sbert.net/examples/cross_encoder/applications/README.html)). Hence: "First, you use an efficient Bi-Encoder to retrieve e.g. the top-100 most similar sentences for a query," then rerank those hits with the cross-encoder. The retrieve-and-rerank page explains the accuracy gap. The cross-encoder sees query and document "simultaneously" and performs "attention across the query and the document," but scoring millions of pairs "would be rather slow" [WF] ([SBERT Retrieve & Re-Rank](https://sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/retrieve_rerank/README.html)). The 0-to-1 output describes the classic SBERT example; some rerankers emit unbounded logits.

Anthropic's evaluation follows this pattern. It retrieved "the top potentially relevant chunks (we used the top 150)," gave each "a score based on its relevance and importance to the prompt" using "the Cohere reranker," and kept "(we used the top 20)" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Measured as "1 minus recall@20," the baseline top-20 failure rate of **5.7%** fell to **3.7%** with Contextual Embeddings ("reduced … by 35%"), **2.9%** with Contextual BM25 added ("by 49%"), and **1.9%** with reranking added ("by 67%"). The post also found that "Passing the top-20 chunks to the model is more effective than just the top-10 or top-5." It warns that "more information can be distracting for models so there's a limit to this," and that reranking "inevitably adds a small amount of latency." These are **relative reductions on Anthropic's own datasets**, with 2024-era embedding models, Claude 3 Haiku for contextualization, and only the Cohere reranker tested. The datasets and embedding models used were not captured. The before/after percentages appeared in fetch output but not as a single verbatim sentence.

## Prompt assembly and citations diverge sharply by provider

Every provider follows two steps. First, place the retrieved text in context, clearly delimited and labeled with its source. Second, return machine-checkable pointers from the answer back to source spans. The field names and guarantees differ, and they change.

**Anthropic** gives the most explicit prompt-layout advice. The old long-context-tips URL redirects (302) into "Prompting best practices" under "Long context prompting." That page has no date and lists current models including Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5 and Fable 5.1 ([Anthropic Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). For inputs of "20k+ tokens," it says: "Place your long documents and inputs near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples." It reports that "Queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs." That is a vendor-reported figure with no published methodology in the notes. Developers should "wrap each document in `<document>` tags with `<document_content>` and `<source>` (and other metadata) subtags," inside a `<documents>` wrapper with `index` attributes. They should "ask Claude to quote relevant parts of the documents first before carrying out its task," using `<quotes>` tags. This advice fits Lost in the Middle's recency finding, but Anthropic does not cite that paper. Present the link as a connection, not as Anthropic's stated reason.

Anthropic's **Citations API** (GA on the Claude API, Claude Platform on AWS, Bedrock, Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry; "All active models support citations"; no date) moves grounding into the API itself ([Anthropic Citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations)). "Citations return the exact passages that support each claim, so you can verify answers and surface sources to your users." It is enabled per document with `"citations": {"enabled": true}`, and optional `title` and `context` fields. "Document contents are 'chunked' to define the minimum granularity of possible citations." Plain text and PDFs "are automatically chunked into sentences," while custom content blocks "are used as-is and no further chunking is done." The RAG-specific guidance is: "if you want Claude to be able to cite specific sentences from your RAG chunks, you should put each RAG chunk into a plain text document." Locations come back as `char_location`, `page_location` or `content_block_location` (0-indexed). The key guarantee is that "citations are guaranteed to contain valid pointers to the provided documents." Anthropic also says they are "significantly more likely to cite the most relevant quotes from documents than purely prompt-based approaches," with no number given. `cited_text` "does not count toward output tokens," and enabling citations adds "a slight increase in input tokens." There are two documented limits. "Citations cannot be used together with structured outputs" (a 400 error), and "Citing images from PDFs is not currently supported." Citations also work on `search_result` blocks ([Claude Docs: Search results](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/search-results)). A valid pointer guarantees that the cited span exists, not that the claim it supports is correct.

**OpenAI's** file search returns a `file_search_call` item plus a `message` whose annotations include `"type": "file_citation"` entries with `file_id` and `filename`. "The file search call will not return search results by default"; raw results require `include: ["file_search_call.results"]` [WF] ([OpenAI File search](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-file-search)). Results can be filtered: "You can filter the search results based on the metadata of the files." For the standalone Retrieval API, semantic search "leverages vector embeddings to surface semantically relevant results." You then "synthesize a response" by supplying results and query "to get back a grounded response" [WF] ([OpenAI Retrieval](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval)).

**Google** defines the concept most broadly. "In generative AI, grounding is the ability to connect model output to verifiable sources of information." The listed benefits are that it "Reduces model hallucinations," "Anchors model responses to your data sources," and "Provides auditability by providing grounding support, which are links to sources" [WF] ([Google Cloud Grounding overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/grounding/overview); "Last updated 2026-10-15 UTC"). Sources include Google Search, Google Maps, Agent Search, RAG Engine, Elasticsearch, "your search API," Web Grounding for Enterprise and Parallel web search. So for Google, RAG is one route to grounding (the note-takers' inference from usage). Grounding with Google Search "connects the Gemini model to real-time web content and works with all available languages." It helps "Reduce model hallucinations by basing responses on real-world information" and lets the model "cite verifiable sources beyond its knowledge cutoff." The model "automatically generates one or multiple search queries and executes them" ([Gemini Google Search grounding](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/google-search); "Last updated 2026-10-13 UTC"). The current page documents the Interactions API with `url_citation` annotations carrying `url`, `title`, `start_index` and `end_index`: "Each annotation includes `start_index` and `end_index` to identify which part of the text it cites." "Older models use a `google_search_retrieval` tool," and the older `groundingMetadata`/`groundingSupports` naming is absent from the current page. Whether `generateContent` still returns those fields was not checked. Gemini File Search likewise returns citation annotations ([Gemini File Search](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/file-search)).

## Documented failures occur at two layers: retrieval misses and reading misses

The first layer is **retrieval failure**: the right chunk never reaches the model. Anthropic measured it directly. Even its best configuration missed relevant chunks 1.9% of the time at top-20, against 5.7% for the baseline ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Context lost during chunking is the main cause it names. OpenAI documents the recall-versus-cost knob. Lowering `max_num_results` "can help reduce both token usage and latency, but may come at the cost of reduced answer quality" ([OpenAI File search](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-file-search)). A higher `score_threshold` admits only more relevant chunks but can exclude useful ones (paraphrased) ([OpenAI Retrieval](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval)). The original paper documents a training-time version: the retriever can "collapse" and "learn to retrieve the same documents regardless of the input" on some tasks ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). Silent embedding truncation [WF] ([HF Cookbook](https://huggingface.co/learn/cookbook/advanced_rag)) and unnormalized vectors in an inner-product index ([FAISS wiki](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/MetricType-and-distances)) are engineering causes of the same failure.

The second layer is **reading failure**: the chunk is present but ignored or misused. Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (arXiv 2307.03172, v1 6 Jul 2023, v3 20 Nov 2023; TACL Vol. 12, pp. 157–173, 2024, DOI 10.1162/tacl_a_00638 per the [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/)), found that "performance is often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context" ([arXiv 2307.03172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). The authors "observe a distinctive U-shaped performance curve," with "primacy bias" and "recency bias," on "multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval," using NaturalQuestions-Open with "10, 20, and 30 total documents" ([v3 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03172v3)). The striking result is that with the answer mid-context, GPT-3.5-Turbo scored "lower than its performance when predicting without any documents (i.e., the closed-book setting; 56.1%)." Performance "can drop by more than 20%." "Extended-context models are not necessarily better at using their input context." More retrieval quickly stops helping: "using 50 documents instead of 20 retrieved documents only marginally improves performance (∼1.5% for GPT-3.5-Turbo and ∼1% for claude-1.3)," because "model performance saturates long before retriever recall saturates." Placing the query both before and after the data "enables near-perfect performance on the synthetic key-value task, but minimally changes trends in multi-document QA." The models tested were LongChat-13B (16K), MPT-30B-Instruct, GPT-3.5-Turbo (and 16K) and Claude-1.3 (and 100K). These are 2023-era models, so **teach this as a documented phenomenon to test for, not a fixed property of current frontier models.** Newer replication studies were out of scope for this pass.

Hallucination persists even with RAG, and the primary sources say so. Lewis et al. report that "RAG models hallucinate less and generate factually correct text more often than BART" (Sec 4.2). Their Broader Impact section adds that Wikipedia-grounded generation "will probably never be entirely factual," and warns that the system "might be used to generate abuse, faked or misleading content in the news or on social media" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.11401)). Anthropic is the most explicit provider: "while these techniques significantly reduce hallucinations, they don't eliminate them entirely. Always validate critical information." Models "can sometimes generate text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the given context," meaning they can contradict documents they were given ([Anthropic Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). Its mitigations are to "Allow Claude to say 'I don't know'," extract "word-for-word quotes first" for documents over 20k tokens, instruct "If it can't find a quote, it must retract the claim," and "only use information from provided documents." Google says grounding "reduces" hallucinations, which implies but does not state non-elimination. **No OpenAI or Google page fetched explicitly says grounding does not eliminate hallucination**, and no provider gives an end-to-end hallucination rate for its RAG product. Rerankers and hybrid search address the first layer. Document ordering, tighter top-k after reranking, and quote-first prompting address the second (the note-takers' synthesis).

## Conclusion

For teaching, the main point is that "RAG" now names an architecture *pattern* whose authoritative pieces are older than the term. BM25 (2009), RRF (2009), HNSW (2016–2018), FAISS (2017), SBERT (2019) and DPR (2020) are all stable and attributable. Lewis et al. contributed the idea of retrieval as differentiable non-parametric memory, and that idea did not survive into production practice: no provider documents joint retriever-generator training. A lesson that opens with the Lewis architecture should therefore say plainly that today's systems keep its retrieve-then-condition shape and its frozen-index economics, while dropping the joint training. The parts that change often (chunk defaults, embedding model names, citation field names, product branding) are exactly the provider-specific parts, and they should appear in the lesson with a date and a link, never as constants.

The evidence also suggests a better order for the lesson than "chunk → embed → retrieve → generate." The primary sources group cleanly around two separately measurable failures: recall@k for retrieval (Anthropic) and position-dependent use of retrieved text (Liu et al.). Hybrid search, contextualization and reranking address the first; ordering, tight top-k and quote-first or API-level citations address the second. Neither layer reaches zero error in any cited source. The most useful Pass 2 work is to character-check the [WF] excerpts, capture Anthropic's evaluation datasets, confirm OpenAI's `file_search` result default, and check whether Lost in the Middle still replicates on 2026 long-context models, since that one study carries most of the lesson's claims about reading failures.
